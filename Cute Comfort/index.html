Emotional Data Ownership in 2046

Nailoong caught me.
Then what?

A failed exam, a late shift, a message left on read — you send a goofy little yellow dragon instead of explaining yourself, and somehow it helps. But when that comfort moves into an AI that logs and infers, being caught starts to come with a second bill.

Core argument: even if people hold more rights over their personal data in 2046, platforms may still control how that data is interpreted and used. Having rights over data does not always mean having real control over it.

21:47

21:50

TOMORROW

From next time on it "gets" you better — and knows better what to show you.

The question isn't whether it comforted me. It's: who gets to decide what my bad day is eventually turned into.
02 / RELATIONAL WORK

A sticker does the job better than "I'm not okay"

It isn't childish. It's a fairly sophisticated social technique.

🫂

Laughing together is enough

Forwarding the same image completes a small collective ritual without anyone having to go first. Exam dread, exhaustion, giving up — all held inside one tilted head.

Durkheim (1995) · collective effervescence
🎭

Seen, without confessing

A joke image is far safer than saying "I'm barely holding it together". Humour leaves a way out for you and for them.

Goffman (1959) · self-presentation
🫥

Too many people, so keep it light

Friends, family, classmates and strangers share one feed; a sincere sentence is hard to aim at just one person. A sticker becomes the safest register.

Davis & Jurgenson (2014) · context collapse

"That exam is done 🐲"

"Don't talk to me today"

"Pat pat. Tomorrow."

But the same image means something else somewhere else. Costa (2018) reminds us that affordances are shaped in practice, not fixed by technology alone; Zelizer's (2005) "relational work" puts it more plainly: the same forward means care between friends, while inside a commercial chatbot it can serve a very different purpose. Research on dating apps likewise shows platforms reorganising intimate interaction through market logics without removing emotional uncertainty (Bandinelli & Gandini, 2022). So we treat Nailoong as meaningful social interaction, not simply a hook for harvesting information. The real question is what happens when that relational practice moves into a system that measures and predicts its users — which is exactly what the live demo below does.

04 / THREE LAYERS

From "I sent a sticker" to "the system says what kind of person I am"

van Dijck (2014) calls the translation of everyday life into measurable traces datafication. Scroll the right column.

CURRENTLY READING L1 · What you handed over

Words, images, voice notes, that "I'm fine". The layer most easily mistaken for "all the data there is".

L1

What you handed over

The words, images, voice notes, that "I'm fine". Visible, legible, and the only layer you can usually download, correct or delete.

L2

What you left behind

When you opened it, how long you stayed, the fourth night running, which line made you reply, which line you deleted. You never "said" any of it; all of it was logged.

L3

What was inferred ⚠

An estimated stress level, a preferred comfort style, a label like "low resilience". It comes from nothing you actually said, yet it is the layer most likely to shape what you see next.

Why layer three matters: an inference can be inaccurate and still change someone's opportunities, their feed, and how others treat them. A "low resilience" score is a model's judgement, not an objective description of the student.

Identity gets rewritten along the way. Rowland & Estevens (2025) show that users understand digital identity in very different ways, with real consequences for agency and control; Bhandari & Bimo (2022) call it the "algorithmized self" — identity increasingly shaped through interaction with recommendation systems. If an AI companion keeps serving one particular kind of comfort, it is taking part in shaping how you understand and express your feelings. That is also rationalisation in Weber's (1978) sense: complex emotion compressed into categories, probabilities and efficient decisions. Beer (2017) and Burrell & Fourcade (2021) add that algorithmic social power sits precisely in which classifications it makes ordinary.

06 / WHO OWNS, WHO CONTROLS

"Ownership" hides several unrelated claims

Spread them out and "whoever stores the file owns it" stops being enough.

IP HOLDER

Character rights holder

Controls licensed artwork and use of the image. That does not automatically give them rights over a student's emotional history.

Controls: the image
PLATFORM

The platform

Holds the messages and behavioural records, and decides what the interface looks like and what counts as "a successful comfort".

Controls: storage & setting
AI VENDOR

The AI company

Owns the models, methods and inference rules. It's hard to find out how a label like "low resilience" was actually calculated.

Controls: interpretation
YOU

You

Care most about privacy, and have the strongest reason to question how sensitive information is used — yet can usually only download a chat log and delete an account.

Should hold: an enforceable bundle of powers
Current European data protection offers qualified rights of access, erasure, objection and portability — not an unrestricted property title over all personal information (EDPB, n.d.). You can delete the account; you can't necessarily find out how old messages fed a profile.

Two more hurdles: Andrejevic's (2014) "big data divide" is not about who has data, but about who analyses and who is analysed; Nissenbaum's (2010) contextual integrity adds that a sentence shared for comfort shouldn't automatically become material for commercial targeting — whether a data flow is legitimate depends on its context, actors and purposes, not on a box labelled "I agree". Inferred information linked to a person may itself fall within data protection rules; the hard part has always been making those rights effective inside a complex technical system.

07 / THE COST OF COMFORT

We shouldn't assume all AI comfort is harmful. But a free service still needs a business model.

It can genuinely help someone open up or find support — and still be very good business.

Platforms build value through control of infrastructure and data

Even if the raw chat log is never sold, the platform can sell advertising opportunities against a predicted vulnerable moment. Srnicek (2017) · Zuboff (2019) · Couldry & Mejias (2019)

Who creates the value, who gets the return

Sending stickers, writing messages, replying to others: things done for personal reasons also produce economic value for someone else. That doesn't make every act of friendship the same as paid employment — it means genuine care and commercial extraction can happen inside the same exchange. Marx (1976) · Fuchs (2014) · Zelizer (2005)

The person being assessed changes first

Once you suspect the chat is assigning an emotional-risk score, you may hide certain feelings or learn to write for a better score. The system is then shaping expression, not merely observing it. Foucault (1977)

Classification follows old inequalities

Commercial search and automated classification in public services have already encoded racial and gender hierarchies and amplified harm. These studies don't prove a 2046 chatbot will discriminate, but they show why emotional predictions need scrutiny. Noble (2018) · Eubanks (2018) · Bourdieu (1986)

08 / MEANINGFUL OWNERSHIP

Treat it as a bundle of enforceable powers, not a label

Tick through them to see what that bundle should actually contain (demo only, nothing saved).

One less cheerful caveat: even with a future Web3 data wallet or a personal-data marketplace, the underlying power imbalance doesn't automatically disappear as long as platforms still control the algorithms that interpret feelings. This site is part of the argument too — it lets you talk, logs what that talk becomes, then asks whether your choices counted. It's a fictional demonstration and needs no real sensitive information.

09 / CONCLUSION

By 2046, playful comfort may also become a detailed inference about who you are.

The small Nailoong scene links self-presentation, relational work, algorithmic identity, discipline, exploitation and inequality. Our conclusion: without practical control over interpretation and use, data ownership has limited meaning.

Perhaps there is only one question worth asking: can people be caught, without handing over what their feelings are eventually made to mean?
